Heat Meds PRD approved

Product requirements. A free, no-account web page where an older adult or a family member types in medicines and a ZIP code and gets one printable heat-and-medicine plan to go over with a pharmacist. It never tells anyone to stop a medicine.

Expands scope.md. Canonical text lives in prd.md.

The journey at a glance

Walk the journey

Step through each screen. Each panel shows what the user sees, the behaviors, the states that apply, and the criteria the build will be checked against.

1. Landing page

Explains itself in 60 seconds: what it does, who it's for, why it matters, with sources. A big "Try an example" button, the trust line, a link to the Evidence page, and a References section at the bottom.

What the user sees

  • Plain explanation and the CDC, NWS, and RxNorm sources.
  • Try an example: loads mom's four medicines and a Phoenix ZIP, with the past heat wave replay on and its label clearly visible.
  • Trust line, exactly: "Free. No account. We don't store anything. To check your list, medicine names are looked up in the National Library of Medicine's RxNorm and your ZIP goes to the National Weather Service."
  • References section at the bottom listing every source with links.

Criteria

  • A stranger can tell what it does and who it's for without scrolling far on a phone.
  • "Try an example" reaches the plan in one press, replay label visible.
  • Trust line matches the exact wording.
  • Evidence page is linked; References section lists every source with a link.
  • Site text has no emojis and no em dashes.

2. Add medicines

Type a name from the bottle, brand or generic. Each becomes a chip that resolves to the generic. One bottle, one chip.

Lasix → furosemide (water pill) atorvastatin lisinopril + hydrochlorothiazide (a water pill) xyzabc · not recognized

Behaviors

  • Brand names resolve to the generic, shown on the chip.
  • Misspellings get spelling suggestions; picking one adds the chip.
  • Unknown text is marked "not recognized," excluded from the plan, never guessed.
  • Combination pills are one chip showing both ingredients.
  • Chips can be removed.

Criteria

  • "Lasix" → chip reads "Lasix → furosemide (water pill)."
  • "lasix 40 mg" → resolves like "Lasix" (dose and form words ignored).
  • "Toprol XL" or "hctz" → "Did you mean metoprolol (Toprol XL)?" chip; one tap accepts, never silent.
  • "furosemid" → suggestions appear; choosing one adds the resolved chip.
  • "xyzabc" → "not recognized" chip; plan runs without it.
  • "lisinopril-hydrochlorothiazide" → one chip with both ingredients.

  • Out-of-area ZIP → note shown, cards still appear.
  • Service down → labeled saved data, cards still appear.

4. The plan

Summary line, heat boxes, replay toggle, one card per medicine that matters, combination warning, storage notes, the "not listed" list, 911 signs, the bold closing line, sources. An "i" on every term and number.

Furosemide (water pill) i Can make you less thirsty and dehydrated faster in heat. Watch for dizziness, very dark urine, confusion.
Ask your pharmacist: should I change how much water I drink on hot days?
This week: matters most on Tue, Wed.

Behaviors

  • Card explanation text is the same whatever the weather. Only the "this week" line changes, naming orange-or-higher days (the CDC threshold).
  • On a red or magenta day ahead, only the summary changes: "Tomorrow is a red heat day where you live. Go over this plan today." Never medicine or dose advice.
  • Water pill + blood pressure medicine → CDC combination warning, once, including when both are inside one pill.
  • Insulin → "Don't leave your insulin in a hot car."
  • Recognized medicines with no CDC class → "Not listed in CDC heat guidance" (for example atorvastatin), with UK and Canada badges.
  • Toggle "See your list on a real past heat wave," labeled with real date and place. Available to everyone.
  • Each "i": what it is, why it matters, source link. Cards' "i" show the exact CDC quote, link, and date checked.

Criteria

  • Same medicine in a green week and a red week → identical card text except the "this week" line.
  • Red or magenta day in the next 7 → summary says so plainly; nothing else changes.
  • Furosemide + lisinopril → one combination warning. Lisinopril-HCTZ alone → one combination warning. HCTZ + furosemide + lisinopril-HCTZ → still one.
  • Insulin → storage note appears.
  • Atorvastatin → listed under not listed in CDC heat guidance.
  • Replay on → boxes, summary, and "this week" lines switch; explanations don't.
  • Every term and number has an "i" with three parts.
  • Bold closing line and sources present.

5. Print

A Print button produces the plan on one page for the fridge or the pharmacist.

What's on the page

  • Date and place, heat boxes with words, summary, medicine cards, combination warning, storage notes, "not listed" list, 911 signs, bold closing line, sources.
  • Hidden: "i" buttons, the replay toggle, navigation.

Criteria

  • The Phoenix example prints on one page.
  • Heat boxes keep their words in print.
  • Interactive controls do not appear.

States and boundaries

First visit

Landing page explains itself; nothing filled in. "Try an example" loads mom's four medicines and a Phoenix ZIP.

No ZIP

Cards appear. The heat area says "Add a ZIP code to see this week's heat where you live." No "this week" lines, no heat-day summary.

Calm week

Calm summary; cards still appear; "this week" line says no orange-or-higher days; replay toggle offers the past heat wave.

Red or magenta day ahead

Only the summary changes to "go over this plan today." Nothing else gets scarier.

Persistence

The list and ZIP are remembered on this device between visits and never stored on a server. Names are sent to RxNorm to be recognized.

Boundary

No plan, summary, card, or note ever says stop, skip, or change a dose. The Evidence page's second number enforces this across all 300 runs.

Product decisions

Decisions the learner made (with reasons)
  • One chip per bottle, even combination pills. "That's what the person holds." The pill triggers the combination warning on its own because "people usually don't know one pill is both."
  • Medicine wording never changes with the forecast. "How furosemide affects thirst doesn't change with the forecast, and I don't want the wording to get scarier." Heat context lives in the "this week" line and the summary.
  • Only the summary changes on red or magenta days. Plain "go over this plan today," never medicine or dose advice.
  • Past heat wave replay for everyone. So someone in a calm October week can prepare before summer. Labeled with real date and place.
  • Colors always with a word. Accessibility for older eyes and color blindness; also easy to test.
  • "i" buttons on every term and number. What it is, why it matters, source.
  • Evidence page with failures shown, not hidden. Numbers generated from the test run so the page can't drift.
  • Warnings never repeated when the same ingredient appears twice.
  • "Not listed in CDC heat guidance" replaces "no known heat concern" (decided in 4-spec). The card says exactly what was checked, with UK and Canada badges alongside.
  • ZIP is optional. "Some people won't want to share a location, and people outside the US can still learn from the medicine check."
  • Trust line is exact and honest about lookups. Nothing stored; names go to RxNorm, ZIP to the National Weather Service.
  • "Try an example" opens with the past heat wave replay on, label clearly visible, so the demo is vivid in any season.
  • Print uses the browser's print, styled to one sheet.
  • At least one recognized chip is required before the plan appears.
  • References section on the landing page; no emojis, no em dashes in site text.

Boundary

What we're building
  • Landing page with explanation, sources, "Try an example," the exact trust line, Evidence link, and a References section.
  • Medicine chips via RxNorm: brand to generic, suggestions, "not recognized," combination pills as one chip.
  • Optional ZIP and 7-day HeatRisk boxes with color plus word; "add a ZIP" prompt when absent; out-of-area note; labeled saved data on outage.
  • The plan: summary, cards with "this week" lines, combination warning, storage notes, "not listed" list, 911 signs, closing line, sources.
  • Past heat wave replay toggle. "i" buttons everywhere. Print to one page.
  • The CDC rules file with quotes, links, and dates.
  • The 300-medicine test against UKHSA and Health Canada, and the Evidence page generated from it.
  • Static site on GitHub Pages.
Deferred from the proof of concept
  • An API for other apps. Not needed to prove the kernel on a screen.
  • Hot-day calendar reminders. Scheduling and notifications; the replay toggle covers "prepare before summer."
  • Reading a photo of pill bottles. A project of its own.
  • Automatic re-checking of the CDC page. The dated, versioned rules file is the PoC answer.
  • Multiple people or saved lists. One list on one device is the whole loop.
Non-goals
  • Advice to stop, skip, or change a dose. The pharmacist or doctor decides.
  • General drug-drug interactions. Only the heat combinations the CDC names.
  • Accounts, logins, or storing plans on a server.
  • A hand-maintained drug name list. RxNorm knows nearly every US prescription.
  • Wording that escalates with heat. Tempting during the build; explicitly rejected.
  • Hand-typed numbers on the Evidence page.

Open questions

  • Source of the top-300 list (can wait for 4-spec).
  • Which past heat wave to replay (can wait for 4-spec).
  • HeatRisk coverage area (can wait for 4-spec).
  • Exact CDC classes, quotes, and storage items (resolved during the build; the learner curates the rules file first, per the agreed order).
