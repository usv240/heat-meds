Heat Meds scope approved

Type in your medicines and ZIP code. Get one printable page that says which of your medicines make hot days more dangerous, what this week's heat looks like where you live, and what to ask your pharmacist. It never tells anyone to stop a medicine.

The unique kernel

The CDC asked doctors in 2025 to make a heat-and-medication plan with patients, but patients have no tool to do it themselves. This flips that: the patient or their family drives it, the plan is tied to the real 7-day heat forecast for their ZIP, and every medicine line traces back to a quoted, dated line of CDC guidance.

It names the medicines that matter and also lists the ones checked with no known heat concern, "so it's honest and not just scary."

Who and why

Who it's for

A daughter, around 45, whose mom is 74 and lives alone in Phoenix. Mom takes a water pill, a blood pressure pill, an antidepressant, and insulin. It's June and the next few days are really hot. Today, neither of them knows those medicines change the risk, and nobody has handed them a plan.

The core loop

Type the medicines and the ZIP. In about a minute, get one page to print, take to the pharmacist, or stick on the fridge. Come back when the forecast changes or a medicine changes.

Why it matters to the learner

"Some everyday medicines, like water pills, some blood pressure pills and some antidepressants, make hot days more dangerous, and most people don't know that."

Learning goals riding on it: requirements precise enough to test, and proof on real outside data, not hand-picked examples.

Look and feel

Calm and very easy to read for older eyes: large text, light and dark mode, phone and desktop. Someone who has never heard of this should understand it on their first visit. Honest, not alarming.

The proof-of-concept boundary

In (needed to prove the kernel)
  • liveMedicine name recognition via RxNorm/RxNav: brand or generic to generic ingredient and standard drug class code. Misspellings get RxNorm's spelling suggestions.
  • liveNWS HeatRisk for the ZIP, 7 days, shown as colors.
  • curatedA small, versioned CDC rules file: each CDC drug class mapped to class codes, the reason it matters in heat, the exact quote plus link, and the date checked. Same for combination warnings and storage notes.
  • The one-page printable plan: calm, large text, light and dark mode, phone and desktop.
  • A simple static site on GitHub Pages with a "try an example" button, so judges and real people can try it without installing anything.
  • Clearly labeled saved data (RxNorm lookups for common medicines, one past heat-wave forecast) so the demo still works if a government service is slow or down.
  • testThe 300-medicine run with UKHSA and Health Canada answer keys and the two tracked numbers.
Later (worth doing, not now)
  • An API so other apps can run the same check.
  • A hot-day calendar reminder when the forecast for your ZIP turns orange or red.
  • Reading a photo of pill bottles instead of typing the names.
  • Re-checking the CDC page automatically for changes, rather than by hand with a dated file.
  • Multiple people or saved medicine lists per user.
Explicitly cut (each with a reason)
  • Any advice to stop, skip, or change a dose. The learner's hard rule; the page points to the pharmacist or doctor instead.
  • Drug-drug interactions in general. Only the heat combinations the CDC names are in; everything else is a pharmacist's job.
  • Accounts, logins, or saving plans on a server. Not needed to prove the kernel; the printed page is the artifact.
  • A hand-maintained list of drug names. RxNorm already knows nearly every US prescription.

Notes for later phases

  • A static site means RxNorm and NWS calls happen from the browser with no server. 4-spec should confirm both allow that (CORS) and decide how the saved fallback data is loaded.
  • The CDC rules file, the answer-key comparison, and the two tracked numbers are the natural home for the testable-requirements goal in 3-prd.
